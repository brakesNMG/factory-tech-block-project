# Power Loom
year: 1785
by: Edmund Cartwright
summary: A loom driven by engine or water power, ending the weaver's hand-and-foot work.
tile: 13, 16

## Page: Weaving falls behind
Once the jenny and the water frame made thread cheap and plentiful, the bottleneck swung back to weaving. A hand-loom weaver could only work as fast as their own hands and feet.

## Page: Cartwright's loom (1785)
Edmund Cartwright, a clergyman with no mechanical training, patented a power loom in 1785. It threw the shuttle and beat the weft into place using power from a wheel or engine.

His first design was crude, and other inventors spent decades improving it.

## Page: The rise of the power loom
By the 1820s and 1830s, power looms were common in the mills of Lancashire. One worker could tend several looms, and cloth became much cheaper to produce.

Handloom weavers could not match the price. Their wages fell sharply, and many were pushed out of work.

## Page: Who paid the price?
In April 1826, weavers across east Lancashire rioted and destroyed more than 1,100 power looms. Troops were called in to restore order. Machine breaking had flared before, most famously in the Luddite disturbances of 1811 and 1812.

Skilled weavers lost status and income, and mills often hired women and children to mind the looms.

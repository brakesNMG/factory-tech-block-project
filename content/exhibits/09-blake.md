# Blake: Jerusalem
year: 1808
by: William Blake (poet)
summary: A short poem that asks what the "dark Satanic Mills" did to a green England.
tile: 23, 8
art: lectern

## Page: Literature: the poet
William Blake (1757-1827) was a London poet and engraver. Around 1804-1808 he wrote a short poem that opens his book "Milton". It is now famous as "Jerusalem".

Blake lived as the first mills and crowded towns appeared. He wrote about them as a threat to ordinary people's lives and minds.

## Page: The passage
And did those feet in ancient time,
Walk upon England's mountains green:
And was the holy Lamb of God,
On England's pleasant pastures seen!

And did the Countenance Divine,
Shine forth upon our clouded hills?
And was Jerusalem builded here,
Among these dark Satanic Mills?

- William Blake, "Milton" (1808)

## Page: Technique: contrast and imagery
Blake sets two pictures side by side. The first lines use soft, open images: "mountains green", "pleasant pastures", a shining "Countenance Divine". The last line jumps to "dark Satanic Mills".

This CONTRAST makes the mills look like a stain on a once-holy landscape. Colour words (green against dark) and "clouded hills" turn the factory age into a loss.

## Page: Technique: allusion and metaphor
Blake uses ALLUSION, a reference to something the reader already knows. The "Lamb of God" and "Jerusalem" come from the Bible, and an old legend says Jesus visited England as a boy. "Jerusalem" stands for a perfect, holy city.

"Satanic Mills" is a METAPHOR. The mills are not literally devils, but the word makes machine work feel evil. Critics still argue whether Blake meant real factories, or churches and schools that he thought crushed people's minds.

## Page: Technique: rhetorical questions
Every sentence is a QUESTION. Blake does not state his opinion. He asks whether heaven could really be built among the mills, and the reader feels the answer is no.

The questions make the reader judge for themselves. The last word, "Mills", also rhymes with "hills", so the ugly word lands hard against the pretty one.

## Page: Link to the machines
Arkwright's mill at Cromford (1771) was already running in Blake's lifetime, and the steam-powered Albion Flour Mills in London (1786) were famous. Some readers think mills like these were on Blake's mind.

The poem shows what the machines meant to people who lived beside them, not only how they worked.
